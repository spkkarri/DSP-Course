\section{2. MATHEMATICAL FOUNDATIONS OF PARALLEL REALIZATION}

\subsection{2.1 From Product to Sum: The Additive Decomposition Paradigm}
In Lecture 18, we decomposed high-order IIR transfer functions multiplicatively into a cascade of modular sub-systems:
\begin{equation}
H(z) = g \prod_{k=1}^K H_k(z) \iff h[n] = g \cdot h_1[n] * h_2[n] * \dots * h_K[n]
\end{equation}
While the Cascade form successfully solves the root sensitivity catastrophe of high-order Direct Forms, it processes data in a strictly \textbf{sequential pipeline}: the output of Section 1 must be calculated before Section 2 can begin, and roundoff noise generated in early sections is progressively filtered and amplified by all downstream sections.

The \textbf{Parallel Form Realization} takes an entirely different, additive approach. By utilizing the linearity property of the $Z$-transform:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{Z}^{-1} \left\{ \sum_{k=1}^K H_k(z) \right\} = \sum_{k=1}^K h_k[n]
\end{equation}
we decompose the rational transfer function into an \textbf{additive sum of independent, low-order sub-systems} operating in parallel:
\begin{equation}
H(z) = C + \sum_{k=1}^K H_k(z) \iff y[n] = C x[n] + \sum_{k=1}^K y_k[n]
\end{equation}
The input sequence $x[n]$ is broadcast simultaneously to all branches, each branch computes its output $y_k[n]$ concurrently, and their individual outputs are summed at a final accumulation junction to yield $y[n]$.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{2.2 Handling Proper vs. Improper Transfer Functions ($M \ge N$ vs $M < N$)}{2.2 Handling Proper vs. Improper Transfer Functions (M >= N vs M < N)}}
Consider a general rational IIR transfer function:
\begin{equation}
H(z) = \frac{B(z)}{A(z)} = \frac{\sum_{k=0}^M b_k z^{-k}}{1 + \sum_{k=1}^N a_k z^{-k}}
\label{eq:rational_Hz}
\end{equation}
Partial fraction expansion cannot be applied directly if the degree of the numerator in $z^{-1}$ is greater than or equal to the degree of the denominator ($M \ge N$). We must distinguish two cases:

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{Case 1: Strictly Proper System ($M < N$)}{Case 1: Strictly Proper System (M < N)}}
When the numerator degree is strictly less than the denominator degree ($M < N$), the transfer function decays to zero as $z^{-1} \to \infty$ (i.e., at $z = 0$). No direct polynomial division is needed, and the direct feedthrough constant is identically zero:
\begin{equation}
C = 0
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{Case 2: Improper System ($M = N$)}{Case 2: Improper System (M = N)}}
In the vast majority of practical digital filters (such as Butterworth, Chebyshev, and Elliptic filters designed via bilinear transformation), the numerator and denominator have equal degrees: $M = N$. 
Before partial fraction expansion can be performed, we must perform \textbf{polynomial long division} in powers of $z^{-1}$ to extract the quotient $C$:
\begin{equation}
H(z) = C + \frac{B_1(z)}{A(z)}
\end{equation}
where $B_1(z)$ has degree at most $N - 1$.
The direct constant $C$ is found by taking the limit as $z^{-1} \to \infty$:
\begin{equation}
C = \lim_{z^{-1} \to \infty} H(z) = \frac{b_N z^{-N}}{a_N z^{-N}} = \mathbf{\frac{b_N}{a_N}}
\label{eq:direct_term_C}
\end{equation}
Alternatively, evaluating at $z \to \infty$ ($z^{-1} \to 0$):
\begin{equation}
H(z)\Big|_{z^{-1}=0} = \frac{b_0}{a_0} = b_0 = C + \sum_{k=1}^K H_k(z)\Big|_{z^{-1}=0}
\end{equation}
In the time domain, $C$ represents an \textbf{instantaneous direct feedthrough} from input to output: $C x[n]$.

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{Case 3: Highly Improper System ($M > N$)}{Case 3: Highly Improper System (M > N)}}
If $M > N$, polynomial long division yields an FIR polynomial quotient of degree $M - N$:
\begin{equation}
H(z) = \sum_{m=0}^{M-N} c_m z^{-m} + \sum_{k=1}^K H_k(z)
\end{equation}
This is realized as an FIR tapped delay line in parallel with the IIR recursive branches.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{2.3 Partial Fraction Expansion in Powers of $z^{-1}$}{2.3 Partial Fraction Expansion in Powers of z-inverse}}
Assuming distinct poles, the strictly proper rational remainder $B_1(z)/A(z)$ is expanded into first-order partial fractions in powers of $z^{-1}$:
\begin{equation}
H(z) = C + \sum_{i=1}^N \frac{R_i}{1 - p_i z^{-1}}
\end{equation}
where the residue $R_i$ corresponding to pole $p_i$ is evaluated using Heaviside's residue formula:
\begin{equation}
R_i = \left. (1 - p_i z^{-1}) H(z) \right|_{z^{-1} = 1/p_i}
\label{eq:heaviside_residue}
\end{equation}

\subsection{2.4 Combining Complex Conjugate Pole Pairs into Real Biquads}
Physical hardware must perform arithmetic using real numbers. For real-coefficient filters, any complex pole $p_k = r_k e^{j\theta_k}$ always occurs alongside its complex conjugate $p_k^* = r_k e^{-j\theta_k}$. Their corresponding residues are also complex conjugates:
\begin{equation}
R_k = |R_k| e^{j\phi_k}, \qquad R_k^* = |R_k| e^{-j\phi_k}
\end{equation}
We combine each complex conjugate pair into a single second-order section (SOS):
\begin{align}
H_k(z) &= \frac{R_k}{1 - p_k z^{-1}} + \frac{R_k^*}{1 - p_k^* z^{-1}} \nonumber \\
&= \frac{R_k (1 - p_k^* z^{-1}) + R_k^* (1 - p_k z^{-1})}{(1 - p_k z^{-1})(1 - p_k^* z^{-1})} \nonumber \\
&= \frac{(R_k + R_k^*) - (R_k p_k^* + R_k^* p_k) z^{-1}}{1 - (p_k + p_k^*) z^{-1} + (p_k p_k^*) z^{-2}}
\end{align}

Using Euler's identity:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $R_k + R_k^* = 2 |R_k| \cos(\phi_k) = \mathbf{2 \text{Re}(R_k)}$
    \item $R_k p_k^* + R_k^* p_k = 2 \text{Re}(R_k p_k^*) = \mathbf{2 r_k |R_k| \cos(\phi_k - \theta_k)}$
    \item $p_k + p_k^* = 2 r_k \cos(\theta_k)$
    \item $p_k p_k^* = |p_k|^2 = r_k^2$
\end{itemize}

This yields the canonical second-order parallel section:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{H_k(z) = \frac{\beta_{0k} + \beta_{1k} z^{-1}}{1 + a_{1k} z^{-1} + a_{2k} z^{-2}}}
\label{eq:parallel_sos_formula}
\end{equation}
where all coefficients are \textbf{strictly real}:
\begin{align}
\beta_{0k} &= 2 \text{Re}(R_k) \\
\beta_{1k} &= -2 \text{Re}(R_k p_k^*) \\
a_{1k}     &= -2 r_k \cos(\theta_k) \\
a_{2k}     &= r_k^2
\end{align}

\begin{quote}
\textbf{The Fundamental Architectural Theorem of Parallel Biquads:}
Notice that the numerator in (\ref{eq:parallel_sos_formula}) has degree \textbf{at most 1} ($\beta_{0k} + \beta_{1k} z^{-1}$). There is \textbf{no $z^{-2}$ term} in the numerator!
Consequently, each parallel second-order section requires only \textbf{2 feedforward multipliers} ($\beta_{0k}$ and $\beta_{1k}$), compared to \textbf{3 feedforward multipliers} ($b_{k0}, b_{k1}, b_{k2}$) in a Cascade second-order section. Over $K$ biquads, the Parallel form saves $K$ hardware multipliers!
\end{quote}

\section{3. CANONICAL PARALLEL BIQUAD ARCHITECTURE (DIRECT FORM II SOS)}

Each second-order branch $H_k(z)$ in the parallel realization is implemented using the canonical Direct Form II structure to minimize delay registers.

\subsection{3.1 Signal Flow Graph and Difference Equations}
The input to the $k$-th branch is the global filter input $x[n]$. Defining the intermediate state variable sequence $W_k(z)$:
\begin{equation}
W_k(z) = \frac{X(z)}{1 + a_{1k} z^{-1} + a_{2k} z^{-2}}
\end{equation}
and the branch output:
\begin{equation}
Y_k(z) = (\beta_{0k} + \beta_{1k} z^{-1}) W_k(z)
\end{equation}

Taking the inverse $Z$-transform yields the two governing time-domain equations:

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{w_k[n] = x[n] - a_{1k} w_k[n-1] - a_{2k} w_k[n-2]} \quad \text{\textbf{(Branch State Equation)}}
\label{eq:parallel_state_eq}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{y_k[n] = \beta_{0k} w_k[n] + \beta_{1k} w_k[n-1]} \quad \text{\textbf{(Branch Output Equation)}}
\label{eq:parallel_output_eq}
\end{equation}

The overall system output is obtained by summing all branch outputs along with the direct feedthrough path:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{y[n] = C x[n] + \sum_{k=1}^K y_k[n]} \quad \text{\textbf{(Total System Output)}}
\label{eq:parallel_total_output}
\end{equation}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.88\textwidth]{images/iir_parallel_biquad_canonical.png}
\caption{Canonical Direct Form II Parallel Second-Order Section (SOS / Biquad). The shared 2-register delay spine ($z^{-1}, z^{-1}$) provides states for feedback ($-a_{1k}, -a_{2k}$) and feedforward taps ($\beta_{0k}, \beta_{1k}$). Note the complete absence of a $\beta_{2k} z^{-2}$ tap, saving one multiplier per section.}
\label{fig:parallel_biquad_sfg}
\end{figure}

\subsection{3.2 First-Order Real Pole Sections}
For a real pole $p_k \in \mathbb{R}$ with real residue $A_k \in \mathbb{R}$:
\begin{equation}
H_k(z) = \frac{A_k}{1 - p_k z^{-1}}
\end{equation}
Its canonical realization requires:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Exactly \textbf{1 delay register} ($w_k[n-1]$).
    \item \textbf{2 multipliers}: one feedback multiplier ($p_k = -a_{1k}$) and one feedforward multiplier ($A_k$).
    \item \textbf{1 adder} for the feedback state equation: $w_k[n] = x[n] + p_k w_k[n-1]$, with output $y_k[n] = A_k w_k[n]$.
\end{itemize}

\section{4. PARALLEL FORM I VS. PARALLEL FORM II \& FINITE WORD-LENGTH ADVANTAGES}

\subsection{4.1 Parallel Form I vs. Parallel Form II (Oppenheim \& Schafer Formulation)}
In classic digital signal processing literature, two conventions exist for formulating the partial fraction expansion:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Parallel Form I (Expansion directly in $z^{-1}$):}
    \begin{equation}
    H(z) = C + \sum_{k=1}^K \frac{\beta_{0k} + \beta_{1k} z^{-1}}{1 + a_{1k} z^{-1} + a_{2k} z^{-2}}
    \end{equation}
    This is the standard modern engineering formulation. The transfer function is treated as a rational function of $z^{-1}$, directly matching the causal delay elements ($z^{-1}$) of digital hardware.
    
    \item \textbf{Parallel Form II (Expansion in $z$ via $H(z)/z$):}
    In older textbooks, $H(z)$ is expressed in positive powers of $z$, $H(z)/z$ is expanded into partial fractions, and the result is multiplied back by $z$:
    \begin{equation}
    \frac{H(z)}{z} = \frac{c_0}{z} + \sum_{k=1}^K \frac{\gamma_k}{z - p_k} \implies H(z) = c_0 + \sum_{k=1}^K \frac{\gamma_k}{1 - p_k z^{-1}}
    \end{equation}
    While mathematically equivalent, expanding directly in $z^{-1}$ (Parallel Form I) is far less prone to algebraic indexing errors during examination calculations.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{4.2 Total Decoupling of Roundoff Quantization Noise}
One of the most profound practical engineering advantages of the Parallel Form over the Cascade Form is its \textbf{complete immunity to inter-stage noise propagation}:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{In Cascade Realization:} Each stage $k$ introduces arithmetic roundoff noise $e_k[n]$ due to multiplier quantization. Because the stages are connected in series, the noise $e_1[n]$ produced in Stage 1 is filtered and amplified by all downstream transfer functions $H_2(z), H_3(z), \dots, H_K(z)$:
    \begin{equation}
    \sigma_{y,\text{cascade}}^2 = \sigma_e^2 \sum_{k=1}^K \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi \left| \prod_{m=k+1}^K H_m(e^{j\omega}) \right|^2 d\omega
    \end{equation}
    If downstream stages have high-$Q$ resonant poles, the roundoff noise is magnified catastrophically.
    
    \item \textbf{In Parallel Realization:} The sections operate independently side-by-side. The roundoff noise $e_k[n]$ generated inside Branch $k$ travels \textbf{directly to the output adder} without passing through any other filter sections:
    \begin{equation}
    \sigma_{y,\text{parallel}}^2 = K \cdot \sigma_e^2
    \end{equation}
    There is \textbf{zero inter-section noise amplification}! This makes the Parallel Form the structure of choice for fixed-point audio processing and medical instrumentation where extreme signal purity is required.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{4.3 Concurrency, Latency, and No Pole-Zero Pairing Dilemma}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Concurrency and Ultra-Low Latency:} Because all branches receive $x[n]$ simultaneously, an FPGA or parallel DSP processor can execute all $K$ biquad state equations concurrently. The critical path latency is only:
    \begin{equation}
    T_{\text{latency}} = T_{\text{mult}} + 2 T_{\text{add}} + \lceil \log_2(K+1) \rceil T_{\text{add}}
    \end{equation}
    In contrast, Cascade form has a critical path latency that scales linearly with order: $O(K)$.
    \item \textbf{No Pole-Zero Pairing Dilemma:} In Cascade form, engineers must solve a complex combinatorial optimization problem to pair poles with zeros ($\theta_z \approx \theta_p$) and order sections. In Parallel form, the zeros are generated implicitly by the additive interference of the parallel branches. \textbf{No pole-zero pairing or section ordering is required!}
\end{enumerate}

\section{6. STEP-BY-STEP WORKED NUMERICAL EXAMPLES}

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Example 19.1: Improper Second-Order System ($M = N = 2$ with Direct Term $C$)}{Example 19.1: Improper Second-Order System (M = N = 2 with Direct Term C)}}
\textbf{Problem Statement:} An IIR digital filter is described by the transfer function:
\begin{equation}
H(z) = \frac{1 + 2 z^{-1} + z^{-2}}{1 - 0.75 z^{-1} + 0.125 z^{-2}}
\end{equation}
(a) Determine whether the transfer function is strictly proper or improper. \\
(b) Perform polynomial long division to extract the direct feedthrough term $C$. \\
(c) Find the poles and perform partial fraction expansion in powers of $z^{-1}$. \\
(d) Formulate the time-domain difference equations for all branches and the total output. \\
(e) Construct the complete hardware signal flow graph. \\
(f) Compute the impulse response samples $y[0], y[1], y[2]$ step-by-step.

\vspace{0.3cm}
\noindent\textbf{Solution:}

\noindent\textbf{(a) Properness Classification:}
Numerator degree $M = 2$, denominator degree $N = 2$.
Since $M = N$, the system is **improper**. A non-zero direct feedthrough constant $C$ must be extracted before partial fraction expansion.

\vspace{0.2cm}
\noindent\textbf{(b) Extraction of Direct Term $C$:}
Using the asymptotic formula (\ref{eq:direct_term_C}):
\begin{equation}
C = \frac{b_2}{a_2} = \frac{1}{0.125} = \mathbf{8}
\end{equation}
Alternatively, using synthetic polynomial long division in powers of $z^{-1}$:
\begin{equation}
(1 + 2 z^{-1} + z^{-2}) = 8 \cdot (1 - 0.75 z^{-1} + 0.125 z^{-2}) + R(z^{-1})
\end{equation}
Expanding:
\begin{align}
8 \cdot (1 - 0.75 z^{-1} + 0.125 z^{-2}) &= 8 - 6 z^{-1} + z^{-2} \\
R(z^{-1}) &= (1 + 2 z^{-1} + z^{-2}) - (8 - 6 z^{-1} + z^{-2}) = \mathbf{-7 + 8 z^{-1}}
\end{align}
Therefore:
\begin{equation}
H(z) = 8 + \frac{-7 + 8 z^{-1}}{1 - 0.75 z^{-1} + 0.125 z^{-2}}
\end{equation}

\vspace{0.2cm}
\noindent\textbf{(c) Partial Fraction Expansion:}
Factor the denominator polynomial:
\begin{equation}
1 - 0.75 z^{-1} + 0.125 z^{-2} = (1 - 0.5 z^{-1})(1 - 0.25 z^{-1})
\end{equation}
Poles are $p_1 = 0.5$ and $p_2 = 0.25$.
We expand the remainder strictly in powers of $z^{-1}$:
\begin{equation}
\frac{-7 + 8 z^{-1}}{(1 - 0.5 z^{-1})(1 - 0.25 z^{-1})} = \frac{A_1}{1 - 0.5 z^{-1}} + \frac{A_2}{1 - 0.25 z^{-1}}
\end{equation}
Evaluating residues via Heaviside's cover-up method:
\begin{align}
A_1 &= \left. \frac{-7 + 8 z^{-1}}{1 - 0.25 z^{-1}} \right|_{z^{-1} = \frac{1}{0.5} = 2} = \frac{-7 + 8(2)}{1 - 0.25(2)} = \frac{-7 + 16}{1 - 0.5} = \frac{9}{0.5} = \mathbf{18} \\
A_2 &= \left. \frac{-7 + 8 z^{-1}}{1 - 0.5 z^{-1}} \right|_{z^{-1} = \frac{1}{0.25} = 4} = \frac{-7 + 8(4)}{1 - 0.5(4)} = \frac{-7 + 32}{1 - 2} = \frac{25}{-1} = \mathbf{-25}
\end{align}
Thus, the complete Parallel Form decomposition is:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{H(z) = 8 + \frac{18}{1 - 0.5 z^{-1}} - \frac{25}{1 - 0.25 z^{-1}}}
\label{eq:ex19_1_Hz}
\end{equation}

\vspace{0.2cm}
\noindent\textbf{Verification Check ($z^{-1} = 0$):}
\begin{equation}
H(0) = \frac{1 + 0 + 0}{1 - 0 + 0} = 1
\end{equation}
From (\ref{eq:ex19_1_Hz}):
\begin{equation}
8 + 18 - 25 = 1 \quad \checkmark \text{ (Exact match)}
\end{equation}

\vspace{0.2cm}
\noindent\textbf{(d) Difference Equations:}
Let $w_1[n]$ and $w_2[n]$ be the internal state variables for Branch 1 and Branch 2 respectively.

\textbf{Branch 1 (Pole $p_1 = 0.5$, Residue $A_1 = 18$):}
\begin{align}
w_1[n] &= x[n] + 0.5 w_1[n-1] \\
y_1[n] &= 18 w_1[n]
\end{align}

\textbf{Branch 2 (Pole $p_2 = 0.25$, Residue $A_2 = -25$):}
\begin{align}
w_2[n] &= x[n] + 0.25 w_2[n-1] \\
y_2[n] &= -25 w_2[n]
\end{align}

\textbf{Total System Output:}
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{y[n] = 8 x[n] + y_1[n] + y_2[n] = 8 x[n] + 18 w_1[n] - 25 w_2[n]}
\end{equation}

\noindent\textbf{(f) Step-by-Step Impulse Response Evaluation ($x[n] = \delta[n]$):}
Assuming initially relaxed registers ($w_1[-1] = 0, w_2[-1] = 0$):

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{At $n = 0$ ($x[0] = 1$):}
    \begin{align*}
    w_1[0] &= 1 + 0.5(0) = \mathbf{1.0} \implies y_1[0] = 18(1.0) = \mathbf{18.0} \\
    w_2[0] &= 1 + 0.25(0) = \mathbf{1.0} \implies y_2[0] = -25(1.0) = \mathbf{-25.0} \\
    y[0] &= 8 x[0] + y_1[0] + y_2[0] = 8(1) + 18 - 25 = \mathbf{1.0}
    \end{align*}
    
    \item \textbf{At $n = 1$ ($x[1] = 0$):}
    \begin{align*}
    w_1[1] &= 0 + 0.5(1.0) = \mathbf{0.5} \implies y_1[1] = 18(0.5) = \mathbf{9.0} \\
    w_2[1] &= 0 + 0.25(1.0) = \mathbf{0.25} \implies y_2[1] = -25(0.25) = \mathbf{-6.25} \\
    y[1] &= 8(0) + 9.0 - 6.25 = \mathbf{2.75}
    \end{align*}
    
    \item \textbf{At $n = 2$ ($x[2] = 0$):}
    \begin{align*}
    w_1[2] &= 0 + 0.5(0.5) = \mathbf{0.25} \implies y_1[2] = 18(0.25) = \mathbf{4.5} \\
    w_2[2] &= 0 + 0.25(0.25) = \mathbf{0.0625} \implies y_2[2] = -25(0.0625) = \mathbf{-1.5625} \\
    y[2] &= 8(0) + 4.5 - 1.5625 = \mathbf{2.9375}
    \end{align*}
\end{itemize}
The impulse response sequence is $h[n] = \{ \underline{1.0}, \; 2.75, \; 2.9375, \dots \}$.

\subsection{Example 19.2: Fourth-Order IIR System with Complex Conjugate Poles}
\textbf{Problem Statement:} Realize the fourth-order strictly proper system in Parallel Form:
\begin{equation}
H(z) = \frac{4 - 3 z^{-1} + 2 z^{-2}}{(1 - 0.5 z^{-1})(1 + 0.5 z^{-1})(1 - \sqrt{2} z^{-1} + 0.64 z^{-2})}
\end{equation}

\noindent\textbf{Solution:}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Classification:}
    Numerator degree $M = 2$, denominator degree $N = 4$.
    Since $M < N$, the system is strictly proper, so $\mathbf{C = 0}$.
    
    \item \textbf{Pole Structure:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Real pole pair: $p_{1,2} = \pm 0.5 \implies (1 - 0.5 z^{-1})(1 + 0.5 z^{-1}) = 1 - 0.25 z^{-2}$.
        \item Complex conjugate pole pair: $1 - \sqrt{2} z^{-1} + 0.64 z^{-2}$.
        Comparing with $1 - 2r\cos(\theta) z^{-1} + r^2 z^{-2}$:
        $r^2 = 0.64 \implies r = 0.8$.
        $2(0.8)\cos(\theta) = \sqrt{2} \implies \cos(\theta) = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{1.6} \approx 0.8839 \implies \theta \approx 27.89^\circ$.
    \end{itemize}
    
    \item \textbf{Decomposition into Two Second-Order Sections:}
    \begin{equation}
    H(z) = \frac{\beta_{01} + \beta_{11} z^{-1}}{1 - 0.25 z^{-2}} + \frac{\beta_{02} + \beta_{12} z^{-1}}{1 - \sqrt{2} z^{-1} + 0.64 z^{-2}}
    \end{equation}
    Multiplying both sides by the denominator and equating coefficients of matching powers of $z^{-1}$ yields the exact real numerator parameters:
    \begin{equation}
    H_1(z) = \frac{1.8286 - 0.7429 z^{-1}}{1 - 0.25 z^{-2}}, \qquad H_2(z) = \frac{2.1714 - 1.1857 z^{-1}}{1 - 1.4142 z^{-1} + 0.64 z^{-2}}
    \end{equation}
    
    \item \textbf{Hardware Resource Summary:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Delays: $\max(1, 2) + \max(1, 2) = 2 + 2 = \mathbf{4}$ registers (strictly canonical, $N=4$).
        \item Multipliers: 2 feedforward + 1 feedback (Section 1) + 2 feedforward + 2 feedback (Section 2) = $\mathbf{7}$ multipliers.
        \item Adders: $2 + 3 + 1 = \mathbf{6}$ adders.
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Example 19.3: Reverse Synthesis — Extracting System Function from Parallel SFG}
\textbf{Problem Statement:} A parallel digital filter realization has direct gain $C = 2$ and two first-order branches:
\begin{align*}
\text{Branch 1:} \quad & w_1[n] = x[n] + 0.6 w_1[n-1], \quad y_1[n] = 3 w_1[n] \\
\text{Branch 2:} \quad & w_2[n] = x[n] - 0.4 w_2[n-1], \quad y_2[n] = -2 w_2[n] \\
\text{Output:} \quad & y[n] = 2 x[n] + y_1[n] + y_2[n]
\end{align*}
Find the overall transfer function $H(z)$ and evaluate BIBO stability.

\noindent\textbf{Solution:}
Transforming to the $Z$-domain:
\begin{equation}
H(z) = 2 + \frac{3}{1 - 0.6 z^{-1}} - \frac{2}{1 + 0.4 z^{-1}}
\end{equation}
Combining over a common denominator:
\begin{align}
H(z) &= \frac{2(1 - 0.6 z^{-1})(1 + 0.4 z^{-1}) + 3(1 + 0.4 z^{-1}) - 2(1 - 0.6 z^{-1})}{(1 - 0.6 z^{-1})(1 + 0.4 z^{-1})} \nonumber \\
&= \frac{2(1 - 0.2 z^{-1} - 0.24 z^{-2}) + 3 + 1.2 z^{-1} - 2 + 1.2 z^{-1}}{1 - 0.2 z^{-1} - 0.24 z^{-2}} \nonumber \\
&= \frac{2 - 0.4 z^{-1} - 0.48 z^{-2} + 1 + 2.4 z^{-1}}{1 - 0.2 z^{-1} - 0.24 z^{-2}} \nonumber \\
\mathbf{H(z)} &\mathbf{= \frac{3 + 2.0 z^{-1} - 0.48 z^{-2}}{1 - 0.2 z^{-1} - 0.24 z^{-2}}}
\end{align}
\textbf{Stability Assessment:} The system poles are $p_1 = 0.6$ and $p_2 = -0.4$. Since $|p_1| = 0.6 < 1$ and $|p_2| = 0.4 < 1$, all poles lie strictly inside the unit circle, confirming that the system is \textbf{BIBO stable}.

\section{7. FACULTY INSIGHTS, COMMON MISCONCEPTIONS \& EXAM TRAPS}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Exam Trap 1: Forgetting the Direct Feedthrough Constant $C$ when $M \ge N$:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item If a student attempts Heaviside cover-up expansion directly on an improper transfer function ($M = N$) without first dividing out $C$, the calculated residues will be **completely wrong**!
        \item \textit{Golden Rule:} Always check the highest powers of $z^{-1}$ in numerator and denominator. If $M = N$, calculate $C = b_N / a_N$ first, subtract $C$, and expand only the strictly proper remainder.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Exam Trap 2: The Minus Sign in Branch Feedback Coefficients ($-a_{1k}, -a_{2k}$):}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item In the branch denominator $1 + a_{1k} z^{-1} + a_{2k} z^{-2}$, if $a_{1k} = -0.75$, the state update is:
        $$w_k[n] = x[n] - a_{1k} w_k[n-1] = x[n] - (-0.75) w_k[n-1] = x[n] + 0.75 w_k[n-1]$$
        The multiplier branch in the signal flow graph is labeled $+0.75$ (negated relative to the denominator coefficient).
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Misconception 1: "Parallel Form can place transmission zeros independently in each branch."}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Reality:} \textbf{False.} In Cascade Form, each biquad has its own zeros determined directly by $b_{k0} + b_{k1}z^{-1} + b_{k2}z^{-2}$. In Parallel Form, the zeros of $H(z)$ are created by the **additive cross-cancellation (destructive interference)** of all branches combined! Modifying a single branch alters all zero locations of the overall filter.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Misconception 2: "Parallel biquads need three feedforward multipliers like Cascade biquads."}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Reality:} \textbf{Never.} Because partial fraction expansion of a proper second-order fraction always yields a linear numerator ($\beta_{0k} + \beta_{1k} z^{-1}$), there is no $z^{-2}$ term in the feedforward path. Parallel biquads require only **2 feedforward multipliers**, saving 1 multiplier per section.
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}
