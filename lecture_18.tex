\section{2. MATHEMATICAL FOUNDATIONS OF CASCADE REALIZATION}

\subsection{2.1 Why Not High-Order Direct Forms? (The Root Sensitivity Catastrophe)}
In Lecture 17, we studied the Direct Form I and Direct Form II realizations of general IIR systems:
\begin{equation}
H(z) = \frac{\sum_{k=0}^{M} b_k z^{-k}}{1 + \sum_{k=1}^{N} a_k z^{-k}}
\end{equation}
While direct form structures are mathematically sound and straightforward to implement for low orders ($N \le 2$), they suffer from a \textbf{fatal practical limitation in finite-precision digital hardware}: \textit{extreme sensitivity of root locations to coefficient quantization}.

Consider a high-degree polynomial representing the denominator $A(z) = \prod_{i=1}^N (1 - p_i z^{-1})$. When implemented in fixed-point DSP registers (e.g., 16-bit or 24-bit words), each coefficient $a_k$ is quantized to $\hat{a}_k = a_k + \Delta a_k$. 

According to \textbf{Kaiser's Root Sensitivity Theorem}, the displacement $\Delta p_i$ of the $i$-th pole due to an error $\Delta a_k$ in the $k$-th coefficient is given by:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial p_i}{\partial a_k} = -\frac{p_i^{N-k}}{\prod_{\substack{m=1 \\ m \ne i}}^{N} (p_i - p_m)}
\end{equation}
Notice the denominator product:
\begin{equation}
\prod_{m \ne i} (p_i - p_m)
\end{equation}
When a filter has high selectivity (such as a sharp cutoff low-pass or narrowband band-pass filter), multiple poles $p_m$ cluster tightly together near the unit circle ($|p_i| \approx 1$). Consequently:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The Euclidean distance between adjacent poles $(p_i - p_m)$ becomes exceptionally small ($\ll 1$).
    \item Their product over high order $N$ shrinks to near zero: $\prod_{m \ne i} (p_i - p_m) \to 0$.
    \item The partial derivative $\left| \frac{\partial p_i}{\partial a_k} \right|$ explodes exponentially with order $N$!
\end{itemize}
As a devastating result, a minuscule rounding error in the least significant bit of a single coefficient $a_k$ can displace a stable pole inside the unit circle ($|p_i| < 1$) across the boundary into the unstable region ($|p_i| \ge 1$), causing catastrophic unbounded oscillation.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{The Cascade Breakthrough:} By factoring the $N$-th order polynomial into a product of independent first- and second-order polynomials, each coefficient $a_{k1}, a_{k2}$ controls \textbf{only its own two localized roots}. The denominator product in Kaiser's formula involves at most one other root ($|p_1 - p_2|$), completely decoupling the roots and reducing root sensitivity by orders of magnitude!
\end{quote}

\subsection{2.2 General Cascade Decomposition Formulation}
Any rational IIR transfer function with real coefficients can be factored into a product of second-order sections (SOS, commonly called \textbf{Biquads}) and at most one first-order section (if the order is odd):
\begin{equation}
H(z) = g \prod_{k=1}^{K} H_k(z) = g \prod_{k=1}^{K} \frac{b_{k0} + b_{k1} z^{-1} + b_{k2} z^{-2}}{1 + a_{k1} z^{-1} + a_{k2} z^{-2}}
\label{eq:cascade_product}
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $K = \lceil N/2 \rceil$ is the total number of cascaded sections.
    \item $g$ is an overall scalar gain factor extracted such that $b_{k0} = 1$ (or normalized for dynamic range scaling).
    \item Each section $H_k(z)$ has a numerator of degree $M_k \le 2$ and a denominator of degree $N_k \le 2$.
    \item For an odd-order filter, one section has $b_{k2} = 0$ and $a_{k2} = 0$, reducing to a first-order section.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{2.3 Handling Real vs. Complex Conjugate Roots}
Because the physical filter must operate using real arithmetic in hardware (multipliers and adders cannot process imaginary numbers without $4\times$ computational overhead), all coefficients $a_{ki}$ and $b_{ki}$ must be \textbf{strictly real}.

\subsubsection{Case 1: Complex Conjugate Pole Pairs}
Complex poles always occur in conjugate pairs for real-coefficient filters:
\begin{equation}
p_k = r_k e^{j\theta_k}, \quad p_k^* = r_k e^{-j\theta_k}
\end{equation}
Combining their factors algebraically yields a second-order polynomial with real coefficients:
\begin{align}
D_k(z) &= (1 - p_k z^{-1})(1 - p_k^* z^{-1}) \nonumber \\
&= 1 - (p_k + p_k^*) z^{-1} + (p_k p_k^*) z^{-2} \nonumber \\
&= 1 - 2 r_k \cos(\theta_k) z^{-1} + r_k^2 z^{-2}
\label{eq:biquad_denom}
\end{align}
Comparing with $1 + a_{k1} z^{-1} + a_{k2} z^{-2}$:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{a_{k1} = -2 r_k \cos(\theta_k)}, \qquad \mathbf{a_{k2} = r_k^2}
\end{equation}
For stability, the pole radius must satisfy $r_k < 1$, which directly imposes the coefficient bounds:
\begin{equation}
0 < a_{k2} < 1, \qquad |a_{k1}| < 1 + a_{k2}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Case 2: Real Pole Pairs}
If two real poles $p_1, p_2 \in \mathbb{R}$ are present, they are grouped together into a single biquad:
\begin{equation}
(1 - p_1 z^{-1})(1 - p_2 z^{-1}) = 1 - (p_1 + p_2) z^{-1} + (p_1 p_2) z^{-2}
\end{equation}
where $a_{k1} = -(p_1 + p_2)$ and $a_{k2} = p_1 p_2$.

\subsubsection{Case 3: Complex Conjugate and Real Zeros}
Similarly, zeros $z_k = r_{zk} e^{\pm j\theta_{zk}}$ combine into:
\begin{equation}
1 - 2 r_{zk} \cos(\theta_{zk}) z^{-1} + r_{zk}^2 z^{-2}
\end{equation}
yielding real numerator coefficients $b_{k1} = -2 r_{zk} \cos(\theta_{zk})$ and $b_{k2} = r_{zk}^2$.

\subsection{2.4 Modular Signal Interconnection}
In the cascade realization, the output of the $(k-1)$-th biquad serves directly as the input to the $k$-th biquad:
\begin{equation}
x[n] \xrightarrow{\quad} \boxed{g} \xrightarrow{x_1[n]} \boxed{H_1(z)} \xrightarrow{y_1[n] = x_2[n]} \boxed{H_2(z)} \xrightarrow{y_2[n] = x_3[n]} \cdots \xrightarrow{y_{K-1}[n] = x_K[n]} \boxed{H_K(z)} \xrightarrow{\quad} y[n]
\end{equation}

\section{3. CANONICAL BIQUAD ARCHITECTURE (DIRECT FORM II SOS)}

Each individual second-order section $H_k(z)$ in (\ref{eq:cascade_product}) can be realized using any LTI structure. In modern digital signal processors and VLSI ASICs, the \textbf{Direct Form II Canonical Biquad} is universally adopted because it minimizes register count.

\subsection{3.1 Signal Flow Graph and Mathematical Derivation}
The transfer function of the $k$-th biquad is:
\begin{equation}
H_k(z) = \frac{Y_k(z)}{X_k(z)} = \frac{b_{k0} + b_{k1} z^{-1} + b_{k2} z^{-2}}{1 + a_{k1} z^{-1} + a_{k2} z^{-2}}
\end{equation}
By defining the intermediate state sequence $W_k(z)$ such that:
\begin{equation}
W_k(z) = \frac{X_k(z)}{1 + a_{k1} z^{-1} + a_{k2} z^{-2}} \implies X_k(z) = W_k(z) + a_{k1} z^{-1} W_k(z) + a_{k2} z^{-2} W_k(z)
\end{equation}
and the output:
\begin{equation}
Y_k(z) = \left( b_{k0} + b_{k1} z^{-1} + b_{k2} z^{-2} \right) W_k(z)
\end{equation}

Taking the inverse $Z$-transform yields the two fundamental computational equations of the biquad:

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{w_k[n] = x_k[n] - a_{k1} w_k[n-1] - a_{k2} w_k[n-2]} \quad \text{\textbf{(Biquad State Equation)}}
\label{eq:biquad_state}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{y_k[n] = b_{k0} w_k[n] + b_{k1} w_k[n-1] + b_{k2} w_k[n-2]} \quad \text{\textbf{(Biquad Output Equation)}}
\label{eq:biquad_output}
\end{equation}

\subsection{3.2 Hardware Resource Audit per Biquad}
For each individual canonical second-order section:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Delay Registers ($z^{-1}$):} Exactly \textbf{2} registers (storing $w_k[n-1]$ and $w_k[n-2]$).
    \item \textbf{Multipliers:} Up to \textbf{5} multipliers ($b_{k0}, b_{k1}, b_{k2}, -a_{k1}, -a_{k2}$). When normalized ($b_{k0} = 1$), this reduces to \textbf{4} non-trivial multipliers.
    \item \textbf{Adders:} Exactly \textbf{4} two-input adders (2 in the feedback summation, 2 in the feedforward summation).
\end{itemize}

If a non-canonical Direct Form I biquad were used instead, each section would require $M_k + N_k = 2 + 2 = 4$ delay registers. Over a 6th-order filter ($K=3$), DF-I cascade requires 12 delay registers, whereas canonical DF-II cascade requires only \textbf{6 delay registers}—a massive $50\%$ memory savings.

\section{4. PRACTICAL ENGINEERING GUIDELINES: PAIRING, ORDERING, AND SCALING}

While all pairings and orderings yield mathematically identical transfer functions under infinite precision, they exhibit \textbf{vastly different performance} under finite-precision fixed-point arithmetic! Three critical engineering rules must be followed:

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{4.1 Optimal Pole-Zero Pairing ($\theta_z \approx \theta_p$)}{4.1 Optimal Pole-Zero Pairing (Frequency Alignment)}}
A filter with $K$ sections has $(K!)^2$ possible ways to pair poles and zeros.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The Phenomenon:} A pole pair close to the unit circle ($r_k \approx 1$) creates a sharp, resonant peak in the frequency response $|H_k(e^{j\omega})|$. Conversely, a transmission zero creates a deep attenuation notch.
    \item \textbf{The Criterion:} Pair each complex pole pair with the \textbf{spatially closest zero pair} in the $z$-plane ($\theta_z \approx \theta_p$).
    \item \textbf{Engineering Rationale:} The notch from the zero cancels out the sharp resonance peak of the adjacent pole within that specific biquad. This flattens the individual section's frequency response $|H_k(e^{j\omega})|$, minimizing the peak-to-valley dynamic range ratio:
    \begin{equation}
    \min \left( \frac{\max_{\omega} |H_k(e^{j\omega})|}{\min_{\omega} |H_k(e^{j\omega})|} \right)
    \end{equation}
    Preventing high resonance peaks eliminates internal state register overflow, while eliminating deep notches keeps signals well above the quantization noise floor.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{4.2 Section Ordering (Sequencing)}
Once the biquads $H_1(z), H_2(z), \dots, H_K(z)$ are formed, in what physical sequence should they be chained?
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Standard Rule (Low-$Q$ to High-$Q$):} Arrange sections in order of \textbf{increasing pole radius} ($r_1 \le r_2 \le \dots \le r_K$).
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Sections with poles further from the unit circle (broad bandwidth, low resonance) are placed first.
        \item Highly resonant sections (poles closest to the unit circle, high $Q$) are placed last.
        \item \textit{Advantage:} Out-of-band noise and large input signal spikes are gradually attenuated by early sections before reaching the highly sensitive, resonant stages.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{High-SNR Rule (Alternative):} Place the section with the highest gain first to boost low-level signals above the quantization noise floor, provided scaling prevents overflow.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{4.3 Dynamic Range Gain Scaling ($L_2$ and $L_\infty$ Scaling)}{4.3 Dynamic Range Gain Scaling (L2 and L-infinity Scaling)}}
To prevent fixed-point arithmetic overflow at intermediate state nodes $w_k[n]$, a scaling factor $g_k$ is distributed before each section:
\begin{equation}
g = \prod_{k=1}^{K} g_k
\end{equation}
Using the $L_\infty$ norm (peak frequency response) scaling:
\begin{equation}
g_k = \frac{1}{\max_{\omega} \left| \prod_{i=1}^k H_i(e^{j\omega}) \right|}
\end{equation}
This guarantees that for any sinusoidal input with amplitude $\le 1$, no intermediate node in the cascade will exceed unity magnitude.

\section{6. STEP-BY-STEP WORKED NUMERICAL EXAMPLES}

\subsection{Example 18.1: Complete 4th-Order Cascade Realization with Mixed Roots}
\textbf{Problem Statement:} An IIR digital filter is specified by the transfer function:
\begin{equation}
H(z) = \frac{(1 + z^{-1})(1 - \sqrt{2} z^{-1} + z^{-2})}{(1 - 0.9 e^{j\pi/4} z^{-1})(1 - 0.9 e^{-j\pi/4} z^{-1})(1 - 0.6 z^{-1})(1 + 0.5 z^{-1})}
\end{equation}
(a) Determine all pole and zero locations and group them into conjugate and real pairs. \\
(b) Perform optimal pole-zero pairing based on frequency-domain proximity. \\
(c) Order the sections to minimize noise and overflow. \\
(d) Formulate the biquad transfer functions $H_1(z)$ and $H_2(z)$ with strictly real coefficients. \\
(e) Write the complete difference equations for both stages using canonical Direct Form II. \\
(f) Construct the overall hardware realization diagram. \\
(g) Evaluate the first three output samples $y[0], y[1], y[2]$ for an impulse input $x[n] = \delta[n]$.

\vspace{0.3cm}
\noindent\textbf{Solution:}

\noindent\textbf{(a) Pole-Zero Classification:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Poles:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Complex conjugate pair: $p_{1,2} = 0.9 e^{\pm j\pi/4}$. 
        \begin{equation}
        D_{\text{complex}}(z) = (1 - 0.9 e^{j\pi/4} z^{-1})(1 - 0.9 e^{-j\pi/4} z^{-1}) = 1 - 2(0.9)\cos(\pi/4) z^{-1} + 0.81 z^{-2}
        \end{equation}
        Since $\cos(\pi/4) = \sqrt{2}/2 \approx 0.7071$:
        \begin{equation}
        D_{\text{complex}}(z) = 1 - 1.2728 z^{-1} + 0.81 z^{-2} \quad (r = 0.9, \theta = 45^\circ, \text{High } Q)
        \end{equation}
        \item Real poles: $p_3 = 0.6, \; p_4 = -0.5$.
        \begin{align}
        D_{\text{real}}(z) &= (1 - 0.6 z^{-1})(1 + 0.5 z^{-1}) \nonumber \\
        &= 1 - (0.6 - 0.5) z^{-1} - (0.6)(0.5) z^{-2} \nonumber \\
        &= 1 - 0.1 z^{-1} - 0.3 z^{-2}
        \end{align}
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Zeros:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Complex zero pair on the unit circle:
        \begin{equation}
        N_{\text{complex}}(z) = 1 - \sqrt{2} z^{-1} + z^{-2} = 1 - 1.4142 z^{-1} + z^{-2}
        \end{equation}
        Roots: $z_{1,2} = e^{\pm j\pi/4}$ (radius $r_z = 1.0$, angle $\theta_z = 45^\circ$).
        \item Real zero: $z_3 = -1 \implies N_{\text{real}}(z) = 1 + z^{-1}$.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\noindent\textbf{(b) Optimal Pole-Zero Pairing:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The complex zero pair is at angle $\theta_z = 45^\circ$.
    \item The complex pole pair is also at angle $\theta_p = 45^\circ$ ($\Delta\theta = 0^\circ$).
    \item Pairing these two together provides exact frequency-alignment, allowing the transmission notch at $45^\circ$ to suppress the resonant peak of the pole at $45^\circ$.
    \item The remaining real zero $(1 + z^{-1})$ is paired with the real pole quadratic $(1 - 0.1 z^{-1} - 0.3 z^{-2})$.
\end{itemize}

\noindent\textbf{(c) Section Ordering:}
We order from lowest pole radius to highest pole radius (Low-$Q$ to High-$Q$):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Section 1 (Biquad 1):} Real poles (maximum radius $r = 0.6$).
    \item \textbf{Section 2 (Biquad 2):} Complex poles (radius $r = 0.9$).
\end{itemize}

\noindent\textbf{(d) Factored Transfer Functions:}
\begin{equation}
H_1(z) = \frac{1 + z^{-1}}{1 - 0.1 z^{-1} - 0.3 z^{-2}}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
H_2(z) = \frac{1 - 1.4142 z^{-1} + z^{-2}}{1 - 1.2728 z^{-1} + 0.81 z^{-2}}
\end{equation}
Overall transfer function: $H(z) = H_1(z) \cdot H_2(z)$.

\noindent\textbf{(e) Canonical Direct Form II Difference Equations:}
Let $w_1[n]$ and $w_2[n]$ be the internal state variables for Section 1 and Section 2 respectively.

\textbf{Section 1 Computations:}
\begin{align}
w_1[n] &= x[n] - (-0.1) w_1[n-1] - (-0.3) w_1[n-2] \nonumber \\
\mathbf{w_1[n]} &\mathbf{= x[n] + 0.1 w_1[n-1] + 0.3 w_1[n-2]} \quad \text{\textbf{(State Equation 1)}} \\
\mathbf{y_1[n]} &\mathbf{= w_1[n] + w_1[n-1]} \quad \text{\textbf{(Output Equation 1)}}
\end{align}

\textbf{Intermediate Coupling Link:}
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x_2[n] = y_1[n]}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Section 2 Computations:}
\begin{align}
w_2[n] &= x_2[n] - (-1.2728) w_2[n-1] - (0.81) w_2[n-2] \nonumber \\
\mathbf{w_2[n]} &\mathbf{= x_2[n] + 1.2728 w_2[n-1] - 0.81 w_2[n-2]} \quad \text{\textbf{(State Equation 2)}} \\
\mathbf{y[n]} &\mathbf{= w_2[n] - 1.4142 w_2[n-1] + w_2[n-2]} \quad \text{\textbf{(Output Equation 2)}}
\end{align}

\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{images/iir_cascade_4th_order_realization.png}
\caption{Complete 4th-Order IIR Cascade Realization (Example 18.1). Biquad 1 ($H_1(z)$) processes the real poles and zero, feeding intermediate sequence $y_1[n] = x_2[n]$ directly into Biquad 2 ($H_2(z)$), which realizes the complex conjugate poles and zeros.}
\label{fig:cascade_4th_order_realization}
\end{figure}

\noindent\textbf{(g) Impulse Response Evaluation ($x[n] = \delta[n]$):}
Assuming initially relaxed conditions ($w_1[-1] = w_1[-2] = w_2[-1] = w_2[-2] = 0$):

\textbf{At $n = 0$ ($x[0] = 1$):}
\begin{itemize}
    \item $w_1[0] = 1 + 0.1(0) + 0.3(0) = \mathbf{1.0}$
    \item $y_1[0] = w_1[0] + w_1[-1] = 1.0 + 0 = \mathbf{1.0} \implies x_2[0] = 1.0$
    \item $w_2[0] = x_2[0] + 1.2728(0) - 0.81(0) = \mathbf{1.0}$
    \item $y[0] = w_2[0] - 1.4142(0) + 0 = \mathbf{1.0}$
\end{itemize}

\textbf{At $n = 1$ ($x[1] = 0$):}
\begin{itemize}
    \item $w_1[1] = 0 + 0.1(1.0) + 0.3(0) = \mathbf{0.1}$
    \item $y_1[1] = w_1[1] + w_1[0] = 0.1 + 1.0 = \mathbf{1.1} \implies x_2[1] = 1.1$
    \item $w_2[1] = x_2[1] + 1.2728(1.0) - 0.81(0) = 1.1 + 1.2728 = \mathbf{2.3728}$
    \item $y[1] = w_2[1] - 1.4142 w_2[0] + w_2[-1] = 2.3728 - 1.4142(1.0) + 0 = \mathbf{0.9586}$
\end{itemize}

\textbf{At $n = 2$ ($x[2] = 0$):}
\begin{itemize}
    \item $w_1[2] = 0 + 0.1(0.1) + 0.3(1.0) = 0.01 + 0.30 = \mathbf{0.31}$
    \item $y_1[2] = w_1[2] + w_1[1] = 0.31 + 0.10 = \mathbf{0.41} \implies x_2[2] = 0.41$
    \item $w_2[2] = 0.41 + 1.2728(2.3728) - 0.81(1.0) = 0.41 + 3.0201 - 0.81 = \mathbf{2.6201}$
    \item $y[2] = w_2[2] - 1.4142(2.3728) + 1.0 = 2.6201 - 3.3556 + 1.0 = \mathbf{0.2645}$
\end{itemize}
The first three impulse response samples are $h[n] = \{ \underline{1.0}, \; 0.9586, \; 0.2645, \dots \}$.

\subsection{Example 18.2: Third-Order Odd System Realization (1st-Order + 2nd-Order)}
\textbf{Problem Statement:} Realize the third-order IIR system in Cascade Form:
\begin{equation}
H(z) = \frac{0.5 (1 - z^{-1})(1 + 0.8 z^{-1} + 0.64 z^{-2})}{(1 - 0.5 z^{-1})(1 - 0.8 z^{-1} + 0.5 z^{-2})}
\end{equation}

\noindent\textbf{Solution:}
Because the system order is odd ($N = 3$), it must be decomposed into $K = \lceil 3/2 \rceil = 2$ sections:
\begin{itemize}
    \item One First-Order Section ($H_1(z)$) realizing real pole $p_1 = 0.5$ and real zero $z_1 = 1$.
    \item One Second-Order Section ($H_2(z)$) realizing complex conjugate poles and zeros.
\end{itemize}

\noindent\textbf{Section Definitions:}
\begin{align}
H_1(z) &= 0.5 \left( \frac{1 - z^{-1}}{1 - 0.5 z^{-1}} \right) \\
H_2(z) &= \frac{1 + 0.8 z^{-1} + 0.64 z^{-2}}{1 - 0.8 z^{-1} + 0.5 z^{-2}}
\end{align}

\noindent\textbf{Canonical Difference Equations:}
\textbf{Stage 1 (First-Order Section):}
\begin{align}
w_1[n] &= 0.5 x[n] + 0.5 w_1[n-1] \\
y_1[n] &= w_1[n] - w_1[n-1] \implies x_2[n] = y_1[n]
\end{align}
(Requires 1 delay register, 2 multipliers, 2 adders).

\textbf{Stage 2 (Second-Order Biquad):}
\begin{align}
w_2[n] &= x_2[n] + 0.8 w_2[n-1] - 0.5 w_2[n-2] \\
y[n] &= w_2[n] + 0.8 w_2[n-1] + 0.64 w_2[n-2]
\end{align}
(Requires 2 delay registers, 4 multipliers, 4 adders).

\textbf{Total Hardware Count:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Total Delays: $1 + 2 = \mathbf{3}$ registers (strictly canonical, $\max(M, N) = 3$).
    \item Total Multipliers: $2 + 4 = \mathbf{6}$ multipliers.
    \item Total Adders: $2 + 4 = \mathbf{6}$ adders.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Example 18.3: Reverse Synthesis — Extracting Transfer Function from Cascade SFG}
\textbf{Problem Statement:} A cascaded filter has two biquads with intermediate equations:
\begin{align*}
\text{Biquad 1:} \quad & w_1[n] = x[n] + 0.4 w_1[n-1], \quad y_1[n] = w_1[n] - 0.2 w_1[n-1] \\
\text{Biquad 2:} \quad & w_2[n] = y_1[n] - 0.6 w_2[n-1], \quad y[n] = 2 w_2[n] + w_2[n-1]
\end{align*}
Find the overall system transfer function $H(z)$ and evaluate system stability.

\noindent\textbf{Solution:}
Transforming to the $Z$-domain:
\begin{equation}
H_1(z) = \frac{Y_1(z)}{X(z)} = \frac{1 - 0.2 z^{-1}}{1 - 0.4 z^{-1}}, \qquad H_2(z) = \frac{Y(z)}{Y_1(z)} = \frac{2 + z^{-1}}{1 + 0.6 z^{-1}}
\end{equation}
Overall transfer function:
\begin{equation}
H(z) = H_1(z) \cdot H_2(z) = \frac{(1 - 0.2 z^{-1})(2 + z^{-1})}{(1 - 0.4 z^{-1})(1 + 0.6 z^{-1})} = \frac{2 + 0.6 z^{-1} - 0.2 z^{-2}}{1 + 0.2 z^{-1} - 0.24 z^{-2}}
\end{equation}
\textbf{Stability Check:} The system poles are $p_1 = 0.4$ and $p_2 = -0.6$. Since $|p_1| = 0.4 < 1$ and $|p_2| = 0.6 < 1$, both poles lie strictly inside the unit circle, confirming that the system is \textbf{Bounded-Input Bounded-Output (BIBO) stable}.

\section{7. FACULTY INSIGHTS, COMMON MISCONCEPTIONS \& EXAM TRAPS}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Misconception 1: "Cascade realization requires more delay elements than Direct Form II."}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Reality:} \textbf{False.} A canonical cascade using Direct Form II biquads requires:
        \begin{equation*}
        \sum_{k=1}^K \max(M_k, N_k) = N \quad \text{delay registers}
        \end{equation*}
        which is \textbf{identically equal} to the delay count of Direct Form II.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Exam Trap 1: The Minus Sign in Feedback Coefficients ($-a_{k1}, -a_{k2}$):}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item In the denominator polynomial $1 + a_{k1} z^{-1} + a_{k2} z^{-2}$, if $a_{k1} = -1.2728$, students often mistakenly write $-1.2728$ in the state equation.
        \item \textit{Correct Rule:} The state update is $w_k[n] = x_k[n] - a_{k1} w_k[n-1] - a_{k2} w_k[n-2]$. Therefore:
        $$-a_{k1} = -(-1.2728) = \mathbf{+1.2728}$$
        Remember: In the signal flow diagram, the feedback multiplier is \textbf{negated} relative to the denominator polynomial coefficient!
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Exam Trap 2: Does Section Cascading Order Matter?}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Theoretical Answer:} In infinite-precision arithmetic,
        \begin{equation*}
        H_1(z) H_2(z) = H_2(z) H_1(z)
        \end{equation*}
        because linear time-invariant systems commute under convolution.
        \item \textit{Practical Hardware Answer:} \textbf{Yes, order matters critically!} In fixed-point digital signal processors with finite word-length, placing high-$Q$ resonant sections first causes internal state overflow and massive quantization noise amplification. Always place low-$Q$ sections first in physical hardware.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Misconception 3: "Can a single complex pole be realized in a 1st-order section?"}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Reality:} \textbf{Never.} A 1st-order section $(1 - p z^{-1})$ with complex $p = r e^{j\theta}$ produces complex coefficients, requiring complex arithmetic hardware ($4\times$ real multipliers). Complex poles \textbf{must always be paired with their complex conjugates} to form a second-order section with purely real coefficients.
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}
