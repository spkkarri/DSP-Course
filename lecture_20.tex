\section{2. MATHEMATICAL FOUNDATIONS OF IIR LATTICE REALIZATION}

\subsection{2.1 From FIR Forward Prediction to All-Pole IIR Feedback Lattice}
In the synthesis of digital filters, the lattice structure holds a unique and prestigious position in speech processing (linear predictive coding), seismic signal processing, and high-precision VLSI implementations. To derive the IIR lattice, we begin by reviewing the fundamental FIR lattice filter.

Recall that an $M$-stage FIR lattice filter is governed by the forward and backward prediction error recursions:
\begin{align}
f_m[n] &= f_{m-1}[n] + k_m g_{m-1}[n-1] \label{eq:fir_f} \\
g_m[n] &= k_m f_{m-1}[n] + g_{m-1}[n-1] \label{eq:fir_g}
\end{align}
with boundary conditions $f_0[n] = g_0[n] = x[n]$, and overall FIR output $y[n] = f_M[n]$. In the $Z$-domain, defining $A_m(z) = F_m(z)/X(z)$ and $B_m(z) = G_m(z)/X(z)$, the system transfer functions satisfy:
\begin{align}
A_m(z) &= A_{m-1}(z) + k_m z^{-1} B_{m-1}(z) \label{eq:poly_A} \\
B_m(z) &= k_m A_{m-1}(z) + z^{-1} B_{m-1}(z) = z^{-m} A_m(z^{-1}) \label{eq:poly_B}
\end{align}
where $B_m(z)$ is the \textbf{reverse polynomial} of $A_m(z)$, formed by reversing the order of its coefficients.

Now consider an \textbf{all-pole IIR filter} whose transfer function is the reciprocal of an $N$-th order polynomial:
\begin{equation}
H_{ap}(z) = \frac{Y(z)}{X(z)} = \frac{1}{A_N(z)} = \frac{1}{1 + \sum_{i=1}^N \alpha_N(i) z^{-i}}
\label{eq:all_pole_Hz}
\end{equation}
Cross-multiplying yields the input-output relationship:
\begin{equation}
X(z) = A_N(z) Y(z) \iff x[n] = y[n] + \sum_{i=1}^N \alpha_N(i) y[n-i]
\end{equation}
This reveals a profound mathematical duality: \textbf{the input $x[n]$ to an all-pole IIR filter is identical to the output of an FIR lattice filter whose input is $y[n]$}!

To realize the all-pole IIR filter, we must therefore \textbf{invert the signal flow} of the FIR lattice:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The external input $x[n]$ is injected into stage $N$: $f_N[n] = x[n]$.
    \item The all-pole output is tapped at stage $0$: $y[n] = f_0[n] = g_0[n]$.
    \item Rearranging the forward error equation \eqref{eq:fir_f} to solve for $f_{m-1}[n]$ in terms of $f_m[n]$:
    \begin{equation}
    \mathbf{f_{m-1}[n] = f_m[n] - k_m g_{m-1}[n-1]}
    \label{eq:iir_f_inv}
    \end{equation}
    \item The backward error equation remains unaltered:
    \begin{equation}
    \mathbf{g_m[n] = k_m f_{m-1}[n] + g_{m-1}[n-1]}
    \label{eq:iir_g_inv}
    \end{equation}
\end{itemize}
Because $f_{m-1}[n]$ is computed by subtracting $k_m g_{m-1}[n-1]$ from $f_m[n]$, every lattice stage contains a \textbf{negative feedback loop}. The signal traverses the stages in descending order from $m = N, N-1, \dots, 1$, and terminates at stage 0 where $f_0[n] = g_0[n] = y[n]$.

\subsection{2.2 The Step-Down Algorithm (Direct Form to Lattice Conversion)}
Given the denominator polynomial of an all-pole or general IIR filter:
\begin{equation}
A_N(z) = 1 + \sum_{i=1}^N \alpha_N(i) z^{-i} = 1 + \alpha_N(1) z^{-1} + \dots + \alpha_N(N) z^{-N}
\end{equation}
we must determine the $N$ reflection coefficients $k_1, k_2, \dots, k_N$ that synthesize this denominator. This is accomplished via the recursive \textbf{Step-Down Algorithm} (also known as the Levinson-Durbin recursion in reverse).

From the polynomial recursion \eqref{eq:poly_A}, evaluating at order $m$:
\begin{equation}
A_m(z) = A_{m-1}(z) + k_m z^{-1} B_{m-1}(z)
\end{equation}
Since $B_{m-1}(z) = z^{-(m-1)} A_{m-1}(z^{-1}) = \alpha_{m-1}(m-1) + \dots + z^{-(m-1)}$, multiplying by $z^{-1}$ makes its highest power $z^{-m}$ with coefficient 1. The coefficient of $z^{-m}$ in $A_m(z)$ is therefore:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{k_m = \alpha_m(m)}
\label{eq:km_ident}
\end{equation}
To extract the lower-order polynomial $A_{m-1}(z)$, we write the system of equations for $A_m(z)$ and its reverse $B_m(z)$:
\begin{align}
A_m(z) &= A_{m-1}(z) + k_m z^{-1} B_{m-1}(z) \label{eq:sys1} \\
B_m(z) &= k_m A_{m-1}(z) + z^{-1} B_{m-1}(z) \label{eq:sys2}
\end{align}
Multiplying \eqref{eq:sys2} by $k_m$ and subtracting from \eqref{eq:sys1}:
\begin{equation}
A_m(z) - k_m B_m(z) = (1 - k_m^2) A_{m-1}(z)
\end{equation}
Dividing by $(1 - k_m^2)$, we obtain the fundamental \textbf{Step-Down Equation}:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{A_{m-1}(z) = \frac{A_m(z) - k_m B_m(z)}{1 - k_m^2}}
\label{eq:step_down_poly}
\end{equation}
Equating coefficients of $z^{-i}$ on both sides yields the element-wise formula:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{\alpha_{m-1}(i) = \frac{\alpha_m(i) - k_m \alpha_m(m - i)}{1 - k_m^2}, \quad i = 1, 2, \dots, m-1}
\label{eq:step_down_coeff}
\end{equation}
The algorithm executes iteratively for $m = N, N-1, \dots, 2, 1$:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Read the reflection coefficient: $k_m = \alpha_m(m)$.
    \item Compute the divisor $1 - k_m^2$.
    \item Compute $\alpha_{m-1}(i)$ for each $i \in \{1, \dots, m-1\}$ using \eqref{eq:step_down_coeff}.
    \item Decrement $m$ and repeat until $m=1$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{2.3 Schur-Cohn Stability Criterion \& Decoupled Parameter Space}
A perennial challenge in direct form IIR design is stability verification. For an $N$-th order direct form filter, determining whether all poles lie strictly inside the unit circle requires calculating the roots of $A_N(z) = 0$, an $O(N^3)$ numerical procedure that is highly sensitive to roundoff errors.

In lattice realization, stability verification is governed by the celebrated \textbf{Schur-Cohn Theorem}:
\begin{quote}
\textbf{Schur-Cohn Stability Theorem:} An all-pole IIR digital filter $H(z) = 1/A_N(z)$ is Bounded-Input Bounded-Output (BIBO) stable (all poles satisfy $|p_i| < 1$) \textbf{if and only if} all reflection coefficients satisfy:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{|k_m| < 1 \quad \forall m \in \{1, 2, \dots, N\}}
\label{eq:schur_cohn}
\end{equation}
\end{quote}

\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{images/iir_lattice_stability_domain.png}
\caption{Schur-Cohn Stability Criterion and Finite Word-Length Robustness: (Left) Complex $Z$-plane showing unit circle stability boundary $|z| = 1$ and the trivial $O(N)$ lattice boundary check ($|k_m| < 1$). (Right) Comparative analysis highlighting the catastrophic root sensitivity of Direct Forms versus the guaranteed stability and orthogonality of Lattice realizations.}
\label{fig:stability_domain}
\end{figure}

The advantages of this theorem in hardware implementation are monumental:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Decoupled Stability Domain:} In Direct Form, the stable region in coefficient space $(a_1, a_2, \dots, a_N)$ is an irregular, non-convex polytope whose boundaries are complicated nonlinear functions of all coefficients. In Lattice Form, the stable parameter domain is simply the \textbf{hypercube $(-1, +1)^N$}! Each coefficient $k_m$ can be verified and bounded completely independently of all other coefficients.
    \item \textbf{Guaranteed Stability under Quantization:} When fixed-point DSP hardware truncates or rounds reflection coefficients (e.g., into 16-bit Q15 format), the filter is \textbf{guaranteed to remain strictly stable} as long as the quantized value is clamped within $(-1, +1)$. In Direct Form, rounding a single coefficient can easily push high-order clustered poles outside the unit circle ($|p| \ge 1$), triggering destructive self-sustained overflow oscillations.
    \item \textbf{Computational Efficiency:} Testing stability via the step-down algorithm requires only $O(N^2)$ additions and multiplications, compared to $O(N^3)$ iterative root-finding.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{2.4 General IIR Realization: The Gray-Markel Lattice-Ladder Structure}
While the all-pole lattice realizes $H_{ap}(z) = 1/A_N(z)$, practical digital filters require both poles and zeros to achieve sharp cutoffs and notch characteristics:
\begin{equation}
H(z) = \frac{B(z)}{A(z)} = \frac{\sum_{m=0}^M b_m z^{-m}}{1 + \sum_{m=1}^N a_m z^{-m}}
\label{eq:general_Hz}
\end{equation}
Without loss of generality, we assume $M \le N$. (If $M > N$, polynomial division yields an FIR polynomial plus a strictly proper rational transfer function).

In 1973, A. H. Gray and J. D. Markel invented the \textbf{Lattice-Ladder filter structure}, which synthesizes arbitrary numerator zeros without disturbing the all-pole feedback lattice. Their key mathematical insight was that the backward prediction error signals $g_0[n], g_1[n], \dots, g_N[n]$ generated along the lower rail of the all-pole lattice form an \textbf{orthogonal basis} for the internal system states:
\begin{equation}
\frac{G_m(z)}{X(z)} = \frac{B_m(z)}{A_N(z)}, \quad m = 0, 1, \dots, N
\end{equation}
where $B_m(z)$ are the reverse polynomials of $A_m(z)$.

By tapping each backward error signal $g_m[n]$ through a feedforward \textbf{ladder multiplier} $c_m$ (often denoted $v_m$) and accumulating them into a summation bus, the filter output is:
\begin{equation}
y[n] = \sum_{m=0}^N c_m g_m[n] \iff Y(z) = \sum_{m=0}^N c_m G_m(z)
\end{equation}
Substituting $G_m(z) = \frac{B_m(z)}{A_N(z)} X(z)$:
\begin{equation}
Y(z) = \left( \sum_{m=0}^N c_m \frac{B_m(z)}{A_N(z)} \right) X(z) = \frac{\sum_{m=0}^N c_m B_m(z)}{A_N(z)} X(z)
\end{equation}
Equating this with $H(z) = B(z)/A_N(z)$, the numerator polynomial is realized as a \textbf{linear combination of the reverse polynomials}:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{B(z) = \sum_{m=0}^N c_m B_m(z) = c_0 B_0(z) + c_1 B_1(z) + \dots + c_N B_N(z)}
\label{eq:ladder_sum}
\end{equation}

\caption{General IIR Lattice-Ladder Realization (Gray-Markel Structure): The all-pole feedback lattice backbone establishes system poles and generates orthogonal backward error signals $g_m[n]$. Feedforward ladder coefficients $c_m$ tap these signals into an accumulator bus to synthesize arbitrary numerator zeros without altering pole stability.}
\label{fig:lattice_ladder_structure}

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{Derivation of Ladder Coefficients $c_m$ via Reverse Polynomial Peeling}{Derivation of Ladder Coefficients cm via Reverse Polynomial Peeling}}
To find the ladder coefficients $c_0, c_1, \dots, c_N$, consider the structure of $B_m(z)$:
\begin{align}
B_0(z) &= 1 \quad (\text{degree } 0) \nonumber \\
B_1(z) &= k_1 + z^{-1} \quad (\text{degree } 1) \nonumber \\
B_2(z) &= k_2 + \alpha_2(1) z^{-1} + z^{-2} \quad (\text{degree } 2) \nonumber \\
&\;\;\vdots \nonumber \\
B_m(z) &= k_m + \alpha_m(m-1) z^{-1} + \dots + z^{-m} \quad (\text{degree } m)
\end{align}
Observe two crucial properties:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The coefficient of the highest power $z^{-m}$ in $B_m(z)$ is \textbf{identically 1}.
    \item For any index $k < m$, the polynomial $B_k(z)$ has degree $k < m$, meaning it contains \textbf{no term in $z^{-m}$}.
\end{enumerate}
Consequently, in the summation $\sum_{m=0}^N c_m B_m(z)$, the highest-order term $z^{-N}$ originates \textbf{solely from $c_N B_N(z)$}. Therefore, if the target numerator is $B(z) = \sum_{i=0}^N b_i z^{-i}$:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{c_N = b_N}
\end{equation}
Once $c_N$ is determined, we peel off $c_N B_N(z)$ by defining the reduced numerator polynomial $B^{(N-1)}(z)$:
\begin{equation}
B^{(N-1)}(z) = B(z) - c_N B_N(z)
\end{equation}
The polynomial $B^{(N-1)}(z)$ is guaranteed to have degree at most $N-1$. Repeating this argument for $m = N-1, N-2, \dots, 1, 0$:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{c_m = \text{Coefficient of } z^{-m} \text{ in } B^{(m)}(z)}
\label{eq:cm_peel}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{B^{(m-1)}(z) = B^{(m)}(z) - c_m B_m(z)}
\label{eq:poly_peel}
\end{equation}
with $B^{(N)}(z) \equiv B(z)$. At the final step $m=0$, $c_0 = B^{(0)}(z)$, since $B_0(z) = 1$. This recursive projection uniquely and exactly determines all ladder tap weights $c_m$!

\subsection{Key Architectural Trade-offs}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Canonical Storage ($N$ Delay Registers):} Although the Lattice-Ladder structure possesses both a feedback lattice and a feedforward ladder, it requires only \textbf{$N$ delay registers} $z^{-1}$, exactly matching the theoretical lower bound for an $N$-th order system. The states stored in these $N$ registers simultaneously drive the all-pole feedback loop and feed the ladder taps.
    \item \textbf{Multiplier Overhead ($3N + 1$):} The Lattice-Ladder requires $2N$ reflection multipliers ($k_m$) plus $N + 1$ ladder multipliers ($c_m$), totaling $3N + 1$ multipliers. This is higher than Direct Form II ($2N + 1$). However, in silicon implementations, the silicon area spent on multipliers is negligible compared to the massive word-length reductions enabled by lattice robustness:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item A Direct Form filter often requires 24 to 32 bits of coefficient precision to avoid shifting poles into instability.
        \item The Lattice-Ladder filter routinely achieves identical or superior frequency selectivity using only \textbf{12 to 16 bits}!
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Absence of Zero-Input Overflow Limit Cycles:} By scaling internal nodes using normalized lattice or wave digital filter principles, lattice structures satisfy the \textbf{Lyapunov stability criterion}:
    \begin{equation}
    \sum_{m=0}^N |f_m[n]|^2 + |g_m[n]|^2 \le \sum_{m=0}^N |f_m[n-1]|^2 + |g_m[n-1]|^2
    \end{equation}
    Energy is strictly dissipative under saturation arithmetic, completely eliminating parasitic autonomous limit-cycle oscillations.
    \item \textbf{Systolic VLSI Pipelining:} Unlike Direct Form structures which feature global feedback accumulation rails of length $N$, lattice stages communicate solely with nearest neighbors. This local communication topology makes lattice filters ideal for high-throughput \textbf{systolic array pipelining}.
\end{enumerate}

\section{4. STEP-BY-STEP WORKED NUMERICAL EXAMPLES}

\subsection{Example 20.1: All-Pole Lattice Step-Down Analysis \& Stability Verification}
\textbf{Problem Statement:} An all-pole IIR digital filter is specified by the denominator polynomial:
\begin{equation}
A_3(z) = 1 - 0.9 z^{-1} + 0.64 z^{-2} - 0.5 z^{-3}
\end{equation}
Determine the reflection coefficients $k_1, k_2, k_3$, evaluate filter stability using the Schur-Cohn criterion, and write the state difference equations for all lattice stages.

\vspace{1mm}
\noindent\textbf{Step 1: Stage 3 Analysis ($m = 3$)}
From $A_3(z)$, the direct form coefficients are:
\begin{equation}
\alpha_3(1) = -0.9, \quad \alpha_3(2) = 0.64, \quad \alpha_3(3) = -0.5
\end{equation}
The reflection coefficient is:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{k_3 = \alpha_3(3) = -0.5}
\end{equation}
Compute the denominator factor:
\begin{equation}
1 - k_3^2 = 1 - (-0.5)^2 = 1 - 0.25 = 0.75
\end{equation}
Apply the step-down formula \eqref{eq:step_down_coeff} to compute $\alpha_2(1)$ and $\alpha_2(2)$:
\begin{align}
\alpha_2(1) &= \frac{\alpha_3(1) - k_3 \alpha_3(2)}{1 - k_3^2} = \frac{-0.9 - (-0.5)(0.64)}{0.75} = \frac{-0.9 + 0.32}{0.75} = \frac{-0.58}{0.75} \approx \mathbf{-0.7733} \\
\alpha_2(2) &= \frac{\alpha_3(2) - k_3 \alpha_3(1)}{1 - k_3^2} = \frac{0.64 - (-0.5)(-0.9)}{0.75} = \frac{0.64 - 0.45}{0.75} = \frac{0.19}{0.75} \approx \mathbf{0.2533}
\end{align}
The reduced second-order polynomial is:
\begin{equation}
A_2(z) = 1 - 0.7733 z^{-1} + 0.2533 z^{-2}
\end{equation}

\vspace{1mm}
\noindent\textbf{Step 2: Stage 2 Analysis ($m = 2$)}
From $A_2(z)$, the reflection coefficient is:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{k_2 = \alpha_2(2) = 0.2533}
\end{equation}
Compute the denominator factor:
\begin{equation}
1 - k_2^2 = 1 - (0.2533)^2 = 1 - 0.06418 = 0.93582
\end{equation}
Apply the step-down formula to compute $\alpha_1(1)$:
\begin{equation}
\alpha_1(1) = \frac{\alpha_2(1) - k_2 \alpha_2(1)}{1 - k_2^2} = \frac{\alpha_2(1)(1 - k_2)}{1 - k_2^2} = \frac{\alpha_2(1)}{1 + k_2}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\alpha_1(1) = \frac{-0.77333}{1 + 0.25333} = \frac{-0.77333}{1.25333} \approx \mathbf{-0.6170}
\end{equation}
The reduced first-order polynomial is:
\begin{equation}
A_1(z) = 1 - 0.6170 z^{-1}
\end{equation}

\vspace{1mm}
\noindent\textbf{Step 3: Stage 1 Analysis ($m = 1$)}
From $A_1(z)$, the reflection coefficient is:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{k_1 = \alpha_1(1) = -0.6170}
\end{equation}

\vspace{1mm}
\noindent\textbf{Step 4: Schur-Cohn Stability Verification}
We examine the magnitudes of all reflection coefficients:
\begin{align}
|k_1| &= |-0.6170| = 0.6170 < 1 \\
|k_2| &= |0.2533| = 0.2533 < 1 \\
|k_3| &= |-0.5000| = 0.5000 < 1
\end{align}
Because $|k_m| < 1$ for all $m \in \{1, 2, 3\}$, the Schur-Cohn criterion is strictly satisfied. \textbf{The filter is unconditionally BIBO stable}, with all 3 poles residing strictly inside the unit circle $|z| < 1$.

\vspace{1mm}
\noindent\textbf{Step 5: Lattice State Difference Equations}
With $x[n] = f_3[n]$, the real-time sample-by-sample update equations are:
\begin{align}
f_2[n] &= f_3[n] - k_3 g_2[n-1] = x[n] - (-0.5) g_2[n-1] = x[n] + 0.5 g_2[n-1] \\
g_3[n] &= k_3 f_2[n] + g_2[n-1] = -0.5 f_2[n] + g_2[n-1] \\
f_1[n] &= f_2[n] - k_2 g_1[n-1] = f_2[n] - 0.2533 g_1[n-1] \\
g_2[n] &= k_2 f_1[n] + g_1[n-1] = 0.2533 f_1[n] + g_1[n-1] \\
f_0[n] &= f_1[n] - k_1 g_0[n-1] = f_1[n] + 0.6170 g_0[n-1] \\
g_1[n] &= k_1 f_0[n] + g_0[n-1] = -0.6170 f_0[n] + g_0[n-1] \\
y[n] &= f_0[n] = g_0[n]
\end{align}

\subsection{Example 20.2: General Pole-Zero IIR Lattice-Ladder Synthesis}
\textbf{Problem Statement:} Synthesize the complete Lattice-Ladder filter for the 2nd-order rational IIR transfer function:
\begin{equation}
H(z) = \frac{B(z)}{A(z)} = \frac{2 + 3 z^{-1} + 4 z^{-2}}{1 - 0.9 z^{-1} + 0.2 z^{-2}}
\end{equation}
Find the reflection coefficients $k_1, k_2$, compute the reverse polynomials $B_m(z)$, calculate the feedforward ladder coefficients $c_0, c_1, c_2$, and algebraically verify the reconstruction of $H(z)$.

\vspace{1mm}
\noindent\textbf{Step 1: Denominator Step-Down Analysis ($A_2(z) \to k_m$)}
From $A_2(z) = 1 - 0.9 z^{-1} + 0.2 z^{-2}$:
\begin{equation}
\alpha_2(1) = -0.9, \quad \alpha_2(2) = 0.2
\end{equation}
The stage 2 reflection coefficient is:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{k_2 = \alpha_2(2) = 0.2}
\end{equation}
Compute the denominator divisor:
\begin{equation}
1 - k_2^2 = 1 - (0.2)^2 = 1 - 0.04 = 0.96
\end{equation}
Apply the step-down formula to find $\alpha_1(1)$:
\begin{align}
\alpha_1(1) &= \frac{\alpha_2(1) - k_2 \alpha_2(1)}{1 - k_2^2} = \frac{-0.9 - 0.2(-0.9)}{0.96} \nonumber \\
&= \frac{-0.9(1 - 0.2)}{0.96} = \frac{-0.72}{0.96} = \mathbf{-0.75}
\end{align}
The stage 1 reflection coefficient is:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{k_1 = \alpha_1(1) = -0.75}
\end{equation}
\textbf{Stability Check:} $|k_1| = 0.75 < 1$ and $|k_2| = 0.20 < 1$. The system is strictly stable. (Poles are at $z = 0.5$ and $z = 0.4$).

\vspace{1mm}
\noindent\textbf{Step 2: Construct the Reverse Polynomials $B_m(z)$}\\[1mm]
Using the definition $B_m(z) = z^{-m} A_m(z^{-1})$:
\begin{align}
A_0(z) &= 1 \implies \mathbf{B_0(z) = 1} \\
A_1(z) &= 1 - 0.75 z^{-1} \implies B_1(z) = z^{-1}(1 - 0.75 z) = \mathbf{-0.75 + z^{-1}} \\
A_2(z) &= 1 - 0.9 z^{-1} + 0.2 z^{-2} \implies B_2(z) = z^{-2}(1 - 0.9 z + 0.2 z^2) = \mathbf{0.2 - 0.9 z^{-1} + z^{-2}}
\end{align}

\vspace{1mm}
\noindent\textbf{Step 3: Extract Ladder Coefficients $c_m$ via Polynomial Peeling}
The target numerator is $B(z) = B^{(2)}(z) = 2 + 3 z^{-1} + 4 z^{-2}$.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Stage 2 Ladder Tap ($c_2$):}
    The coefficient of $z^{-2}$ in $B^{(2)}(z)$ is $4$. Since the coefficient of $z^{-2}$ in $B_2(z)$ is $1$:
    \begin{equation}
    \mathbf{c_2 = 4.0}
    \end{equation}
    Peel off $c_2 B_2(z)$:
    \begin{align}
    c_2 B_2(z) &= 4.0(0.2 - 0.9 z^{-1} + z^{-2}) = 0.8 - 3.6 z^{-1} + 4.0 z^{-2} \\
    B^{(1)}(z) &= B^{(2)}(z) - c_2 B_2(z) \nonumber \\
    &= (2 + 3 z^{-1} + 4 z^{-2}) - (0.8 - 3.6 z^{-1} + 4.0 z^{-2}) \nonumber \\
    &= (2 - 0.8) + (3 - (-3.6)) z^{-1} + (4 - 4) z^{-2} \nonumber \\
    &= \mathbf{1.2 + 6.6 z^{-1}}
    \end{align}
    
    \item \textbf{Stage 1 Ladder Tap ($c_1$):}
    The coefficient of $z^{-1}$ in $B^{(1)}(z)$ is $6.6$. Since the coefficient of $z^{-1}$ in $B_1(z)$ is $1$:
    \begin{equation}
    \mathbf{c_1 = 6.6}
    \end{equation}
    Peel off $c_1 B_1(z)$:
    \begin{align}
    c_1 B_1(z) &= 6.6(-0.75 + z^{-1}) = -4.95 + 6.6 z^{-1} \\
    B^{(0)}(z) &= B^{(1)}(z) - c_1 B_1(z) \nonumber \\
    &= (1.2 + 6.6 z^{-1}) - (-4.95 + 6.6 z^{-1}) \nonumber \\
    &= (1.2 - (-4.95)) + (6.6 - 6.6) z^{-1} \nonumber \\
    &= \mathbf{6.15}
    \end{align}
    
    \item \textbf{Stage 0 Ladder Tap ($c_0$):}
    Since $B_0(z) = 1$, the scalar constant $B^{(0)}(z)$ directly provides $c_0$:
    \begin{equation}
    \mathbf{c_0 = 6.15}
    \end{equation}
\end{itemize}

\vspace{1mm}
\noindent\textbf{Step 4: Algebraic Verification of Numerator Synthesis}
We verify that the linear combination $\sum_{m=0}^2 c_m B_m(z)$ reconstructs $B(z)$ exactly:
\begin{align}
\sum_{m=0}^2 c_m B_m(z) &= c_0 B_0(z) + c_1 B_1(z) + c_2 B_2(z) \nonumber \\
&= 6.15(1) + 6.6(-0.75 + z^{-1}) + 4.0(0.2 - 0.9 z^{-1} + z^{-2}) \nonumber \\
&= 6.15 - 4.95 + 6.6 z^{-1} + 0.8 - 3.6 z^{-1} + 4.0 z^{-2} \nonumber \\
&= (6.15 - 4.95 + 0.8) + (6.6 - 3.6) z^{-1} + 4.0 z^{-2} \nonumber \\
&= \mathbf{2 + 3 z^{-1} + 4 z^{-2}} \equiv B(z) \quad \text{\textbf{(Q.E.D.)}}
\end{align}
The synthesized Lattice-Ladder filter matches the exact specification!

\subsection{Example 20.3: Reverse Analysis (Synthesis from Lattice Parameters)}
\textbf{Problem Statement:} A 2-stage Lattice-Ladder filter is implemented in DSP hardware with reflection coefficients:
\begin{equation}
k_1 = 0.5, \quad k_2 = -0.4
\end{equation}
and feedforward ladder coefficients:
\begin{equation}
c_0 = 2, \quad c_1 = 3, \quad c_2 = 1
\end{equation}
Determine the rational transfer function $H(z) = B(z)/A(z)$ of the filter.

\vspace{1mm}
\noindent\textbf{Step 1: Step-Up Recursion for Denominator Polynomial $A_2(z)$}
Starting with initial conditions $A_0(z) = 1$ and $B_0(z) = 1$:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Stage 1 ($m = 1$):}
    \begin{align}
    A_1(z) &= A_0(z) + k_1 z^{-1} B_0(z) = 1 + 0.5 z^{-1} \\
    B_1(z) &= k_1 A_0(z) + z^{-1} B_0(z) = 0.5 + z^{-1}
    \end{align}
    \item \textbf{Stage 2 ($m = 2$):}
    \begin{align}
    A_2(z) &= A_1(z) + k_2 z^{-1} B_1(z) \nonumber \\
    &= (1 + 0.5 z^{-1}) + (-0.4) z^{-1} (0.5 + z^{-1}) \nonumber \\
    &= 1 + 0.5 z^{-1} - 0.2 z^{-1} - 0.4 z^{-2} \nonumber \\
    &= \mathbf{1 + 0.3 z^{-1} - 0.4 z^{-2}}
    \end{align}
    Compute $B_2(z)$ for the numerator summation:
    \begin{align}
    B_2(z) &= k_2 A_1(z) + z^{-1} B_1(z) \nonumber \\
    &= -0.4(1 + 0.5 z^{-1}) + z^{-1}(0.5 + z^{-1}) \nonumber \\
    &= -0.4 - 0.2 z^{-1} + 0.5 z^{-1} + z^{-2} \nonumber \\
    &= \mathbf{-0.4 + 0.3 z^{-1} + z^{-2}}
    \end{align}
\end{itemize}

\vspace{1mm}
\noindent\textbf{Step 2: Numerator Polynomial Synthesis}
Using the ladder summation formula \eqref{eq:ladder_sum}:
\begin{align}
B(z) &= c_0 B_0(z) + c_1 B_1(z) + c_2 B_2(z) \nonumber \\
&= 2(1) + 3(0.5 + z^{-1}) + 1(-0.4 + 0.3 z^{-1} + z^{-2}) \nonumber \\
&= 2 + 1.5 + 3 z^{-1} - 0.4 + 0.3 z^{-1} + z^{-2} \nonumber \\
&= (2 + 1.5 - 0.4) + (3 + 0.3) z^{-1} + z^{-2} \nonumber \\
&= \mathbf{3.1 + 3.3 z^{-1} + z^{-2}}
\end{align}

\vspace{1mm}
\noindent\textbf{Step 3: Overall Transfer Function}
The complete rational IIR transfer function realized by the filter is:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{H(z) = \frac{3.1 + 3.3 z^{-1} + z^{-2}}{1 + 0.3 z^{-1} - 0.4 z^{-2}}}
\end{equation}
\textbf{Stability Verification:} Denominator roots are solutions to $z^2 + 0.3 z - 0.4 = 0 \implies (z + 0.8)(z - 0.5) = 0$. The poles are at $z = -0.8$ and $z = 0.5$. Since both $|-0.8| < 1$ and $|0.5| < 1$, the filter is confirmed to be stable, consistent with $|k_1| = 0.5 < 1$ and $|k_2| = 0.4 < 1$.

\section{5. FACULTY INSIGHTS, COMMON PITFALLS \& EXAM TRAPS}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pitfall 1: Confusing FIR vs All-Pole IIR Signal Flow Direction:}
    In an FIR lattice filter, the input $x[n]$ enters at stage 0 ($f_0 = g_0 = x[n]$) and signal flows left-to-right toward stage $N$ ($y[n] = f_N[n]$). In an All-Pole IIR lattice, \textbf{the input $x[n]$ enters at stage $N$} ($f_N[n] = x[n]$) and propagates through internal feedback loops toward stage 0 ($y[n] = f_0[n] = g_0[n]$). Students who reverse this flow receive zero marks on exam block diagrams.
    
    \item \textbf{Pitfall 2: Sign of the Feedback Term ($f_{m-1}$ equation):}
    In an FIR lattice, $f_m[n] = f_{m-1}[n] + k_m g_{m-1}[n-1]$ (addition). When inverting the flow for an all-pole IIR lattice, solving for $f_{m-1}[n]$ introduces a \textbf{crucial minus sign}:
    \begin{equation}
    f_{m-1}[n] = f_m[n] - k_m g_{m-1}[n-1]
    \end{equation}
    Forgetting this negative sign turns negative feedback into unstable positive feedback!
    
    \item \textbf{Pitfall 3: Tapping Forward vs Backward Residuals in the Ladder:}
    In the Lattice-Ladder structure, the ladder tap weights $c_m$ MUST multiply the \textbf{backward prediction error signals $g_m[n]$}, NOT the forward error signals $f_m[n]$. It is the backward signals $g_m[n]$ whose transfer functions equal $B_m(z)/A_N(z)$, providing the orthogonal polynomial basis.
    
    \item \textbf{Pitfall 4: What Happens When $|k_m| = 1$ in the Step-Down Algorithm?}
    If during the step-down recursion any $|k_m| = 1$, the divisor $1 - k_m^2 = 0$, causing division by zero! This occurs if and only if the polynomial $A_m(z)$ has roots located \textbf{exactly on the unit circle} $|z| = 1$. In that case, the filter is marginally stable (oscillatory), and standard step-down recursion terminates. If $|k_m| > 1$, the filter has at least one pole strictly outside the unit circle and is \textbf{unstable}.
    
    \item \textbf{Pitfall 5: Order of Ladder Weight Extraction:}
    Ladder coefficients must be extracted starting from the \textbf{highest order $c_N$ down to $c_0$}. You cannot compute $c_0$ first, because $B_N(z), B_{N-1}(z), \dots, B_1(z)$ all contribute constant terms to the zeroth power $z^0$. Only the highest power $z^{-m}$ has a contribution solely from $c_m B_m(z)$.
\end{itemize}
